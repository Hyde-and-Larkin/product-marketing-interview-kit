# Offer Review

> **Part of the [Product Marketing Interview Kit](../../README.md).** Read `context/candidate-profile.md` for must-haves and dealbreakers.

**Not financial, tax, or legal advice.** This skill helps the candidate ask the right questions and think clearly. For equity tax questions or contract terms, talk to a financial advisor, accountant, or employment lawyer.

## Before any number is discussed

- Know the role's level and scope. Comp follows level; negotiate the level first if it's wrong.
- Research the range for the level and location from current, cited sources (posted ranges, reputable salary data). Note the date of every data point.
- Write down your walk-away line now: the package below which you'd stay where you are or keep looking. Decide it before you're excited.

## In the recruiter screen

If asked for expectations early, common approaches:

- Ask for the range first: "What's the range budgeted for this role?"
- Defer until scope is clear: "I'd like to understand the scope and level first. I'm confident we can find something that works if it's the right fit."
- If you must give a number, give a researched range whose bottom you'd be happy with.

The candidate chooses; there's no single right answer.

## Questions to ask about the offer

See `offer-questions.md` in this folder for the full list. The essentials:

- Base, target bonus, and how bonus is actually paid out historically
- Level and title, and how leveling works
- Equity: type, amount, vesting schedule, cliff, refresh policy
- Start date flexibility, signing bonus, relocation if relevant
- What's negotiable and what's fixed by band

## Startup equity in plain language

- **Options vs. RSUs:** options give you the right to buy shares at a set price (the strike price); RSUs are shares granted to you. Public companies usually grant RSUs; private ones often grant options.
- **409A valuation / strike price:** the 409A is an independent valuation of common stock, and it sets the strike price, which is what you'd pay per share to exercise. The gap between the strike price and what shares are later worth is your upside.
- **Percentage, not just share count:** ask for total shares outstanding (fully diluted) to understand what your grant is worth as a slice.
- **Preferred stock and liquidation preferences:** investors usually get paid back before common shareholders in a sale. Large preferences can make common stock worth little in a modest exit.
- **Exercise window:** how long you have to buy your options after leaving. Short windows can force a costly decision.
- **Acceleration:** whether vesting speeds up if the company is acquired (single- or double-trigger).

## Negotiation sequence

1. Let them present the full offer. Thank them and ask for it in writing.
2. Ask your open questions before countering.
3. Counter once, on the things that matter most, with a reason tied to scope, level, or market data.
4. If base is fixed by band, ask about level, signing bonus, equity, or a six-month comp review.
5. Agree on a decision date that gives you room to finish other processes.

Keep it warm. You'll work with these people.

## Guardrails

- Verify every company-specific fact (ticker, grant type, bonus norms). Don't rely on recalled figures; they're often wrong.
- Keep the candidate's current comp and offer details in their private copy only.
- Never pressure toward accepting or declining. Lay out the trade-offs.
